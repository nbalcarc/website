## Introduction

I am deeply unsatisfied with many guides on making kombucha on the internet today. Either they spread misinformation based on folk methods, or overcomplicate the process This guide is intended to demonstrate that making your own kombucha, and understanding the basic mechanism behind it, is dead simple.

## SCOBY vs Pellicle

The jelly-like substance on the top of your kombucha is not the SCOBY. It is called the pellicle, and it is made of cellulose. There are almost no micro-organisms within it. The pellicle is not the catalyst for the fermentation, and serves almost no purpose, except possibly as a physical protection from outside contaminants, though we are currently unsure about this. A pellicle can be safely discarded if one forms, don't feel shame about this, I do this every time.

The SCOBY is simply the environment of microbes that live within the kombucha. These microbes are suspended within the liquid itself, and any raw and unpasteurized kombucha (even from the store) contains live and active kombucha microbes. They are invisible to the naked eye, and *they* are the catalyst for fermentation. In this guide, SCOBY, kombucha microbes, and kombucha micro-organisms, will be synonymous.

## Guide

Here's the basic guide for making kombucha. There will be additional notes below the guide for extra discussion.

### Materials

Here's everything you'll need for the most basic kombucha:

- raw kombucha (either from the store or from a previous kombucha batch)
- cane sugar
- filtered water
- loose leaf black tea (avoid any oils like bergamot oil in earl grey tea; I have used english breakfast and assam with success)
- glass jar (slightly larger than the amount of kombucha you want to produce; I think my jar is a little larger than 2 liters)
- small towel (enough to cover the top of your jar, natural materials like cotton are better)
- rubber band
- carbonation-friendly bottles (I use leftover GT's Kombucha bottles, and I do recommend glass)
- fruit for flavoring

Notes:

- Yes, you can just grab a bottle of raw kombucha from the store and start your own kombucha from that. No need to buy a starter online for $20. Any $3 raw kombucha from the store, flavored with fruit or not, will work exactly the same, no need to find an unflavored one, and no need for a pellicle at all. Just ensure it is raw and unpasteurized so it has the micro-organisms you need.
- For your choice of black tea, use anything that avoids additional ingredients like oils or scents, since these can be anti-microbial. Experiment in your own time once you find success however, you may be surprised by results.
- Filtered water is important to remove chlorine and other contaminants that may not play well with the kombucha microbes.
- I would recommend against a cheesecloth, I started off by layering 5 cheesecloths over each other instead of a towel, and let's just say I had unwanted visitors get inside.
- Glass will always be better than plastic, mostly due to food safety, and metals generally are to be avoided as well due to the acidity of kombucha.
- GT's bottles are fantastic in my opinion, because the plastic caps allow you to feel the carbonation levels inside.

### Steps

I'll keep this as simple as possible:

1. Brew 1 liter of tea with 2 tsp of black tea. 
	 - Don't fuss too much about how this is done. Personally, my electric kettle only holds 0.5 liters of water, so I just brew 0.5 liters of water with 2 tsp of tea, and water it down later with another 0.5 liters of water.
2. Wait until your tea reaches room temperature, then add it to your glass jar.
3. Add 1/3 cup of cane sugar and mix.
4. Repeat steps 1-3 until your jar is mostly filled (leave a bit of space at the top just in case).
5. Add at least 1 cup of kombucha to your jar.
6. Place your towel on the jar, secure it with the rubber band, and put in a dark place at room temperature.
7. After 4 days you're gonna wanna taste it, and keep tasting it every few days until you reach the flavor you want.
	- For me that's about 4-5 days, but for others it can be as long as 12 days. Every environment is different.
8. Fill a bottle of kombucha and put it in the fridge, just to have an unflavored backup batch for future fermentation. Should be good for 6 months.
	- Kombucha microbes go dormant when cold, which is also part of why we put them in the fridge after carbonation.
9. We're ready to bottle and carbonage. Do 1 cup of crushed fruit for every 48 oz bottle. Fill the rest of the way with your kombucha.
	- Small GT's bottles are 16 oz, so I use 1/3 cup of fruit for those. The large ones are 48 oz, so I use 1 cup for those.
	- If you have a pellicle, or pieces of one, you can safely discard it in the trash. We don't need it.
10. Let the bottles sit at room temperature for about 2-3 days. Don't burp them. Transfer them to the fridge to halt fermentation and incorporate the carbonation.
11. Once thoroughly cooled, open them, hopefully they didn't explode, adjust the time you carbonate them for accordingly, and enjoy.
	- If you're not happy with the carbonation, let them sit at room temperature again for another few days, possibly adding 1 tsp of cane sugar to speed up the process.

Hopefully this guide is simple enough and clear. Kombucha is highly approachable and easy, and guides online complicate it too much and give too much misinformation. If you're looking for more info, look below at the advanced notes. Happy brewing!

## Advanced Notes and Discussion

Here's some additional notes if you want to expand further or gain extra clarity.

### Throw away the pellicle?

Yeah. I don't like eating it, so I just throw it away. Usually with my ferments, 4 days isn't enough to form a full pellicle anyways, but yeah you can just dispose of them, they don't do much.

Technically people online have found that it could help ferment 10% faster, but that's negligible enough for me to not care. I don't want to manage pellicles, personally.

### Is my kombucha healthy? It looks disgusting!

Yeah it most likely is perfectly fine. Konbucha is made from biological processes, and the process of life is pretty ugly. It may look like there's goo, maybe some bubbles will form, maybe it'll look like there's little tentacles hanging off the surface of the booch. Maybe some brown sediment at the bottom. Pretty much if you think, "I don't know what's going on, but it looks like there's something alive in there," it's because there is. You'll get used to it.

The main time to worry is if it may have gotten contaminated. Usually the acidity of kombucha makes it pretty contaminant-proof, but if there's any fuzziness going on, or any colors other than clear or white or brown, especially any green or blue, then it may very well be contaminated. If you're unsure, give it another 3 days and check again, it may just be the kombucha itself looking weird. If anything gets contaminated, throw it out, wash everything well, and start anew. If you had a backup batch of kombucha, you can continue as if nothing happened (apart from losing your last batch), or buy another raw kombucha from the store instaad and start again.

### F1 vs F2?

So F1 (fermentation 1) generally refers to the time when the kombucha ferments in our jar, and F2 (fermentation 2) refers to when it ferments in the bottles and builds carbonation. I didn't use this terminology in this guide, but you will find it online. There's honestly nothing special about how fermentation happens in F1 and F2. Carbon dioxide is produced in both stages. The main difference is that with F1 we allow the extra gas to escape through the towel, while in F2 we capture it instead. That is all, really.

As for when to add fruit and flavoring, I don't actually think there's a "right" time to do it. In this guide and traditionally you add fruit at the start of F2, but realistically I think it doesn't matter much, but I haven't experimented much on my own. GT's kombucha lists kiwi juice as part of their F1 process, so that's an interesting note.

### Why not burp?

Carbon dioxide doesn't incorporate well into the kombucha at room temperature. The gas will escape, leaving the booch not very carbonated. It gets incorporated better at cold temperatures, so it's better to place the kombucha in the fridge and open it once it's throroughly cooled instead. If the carbonation isn't up to snuff, place it back at room temperature.

### Do you have to use black tea?

No. You can use green tea, oolong tea, hibiscus, maybe even rooibos if you add some true tea to it. Kombucha is not picky. Jun is an adjacent drink that is essentially just kombucha made with green tea and honey instead of black tra and cane sugar.

I personally use about 50% assam black tea and 50% longjing green tea.

### Do you have to use cane sugar?

Again, no. Any source of sugar will work, like honey. There's probably lots of room to experiment here, just avoid artificial sweeteners since they don't have actual sugars in them. Again, jun is made with honey instead of cane sugar, so that's a great option.

### What's the deal with "SCOBY Hotels"?

I didn't mention this in my guide, but you will find references to SCOBY hotels online. In my opinion this seems to stem from the confusion that the pellicle is the SCOBY, and people store pellicles in a jar as a backup or for future use. In reality, if you want to keep a backup culture in case your main culture gets contaminated or something else goes wrong, all you need to do is store a bottle of extra kombucha in the fridge, just like store-bought kombucha. Then just get a cup of that as your 1 cup of premade kombucha whenever you're ready for your next batch. No need to keep anything at room temperature.

### Does the brewing temperature matter?

Life finds a way. It doesn't really matter. If the temperature is good for you, it's probably good for them. But don't let the temperature drop too much, or the kombucha microbes may slow down and other contaminants may gain an advantage. But usually within room temperature range, all that will change is the speed at which your kombucha ferments.

### How long does kombucha stay good for while dormant in the fridge?

This was kinda hard to find info for, and I have not personally tested anything, but a safe answer would probably be up to 6 months in the fridge, with fuzzy results going past that. So if you make a batch of kombucha and store a backup bottle in the fridge, you should be good to use it for another batch 6 months later, maybe more. Just ensure the container is sealed.

### Tell me more about jun...

Okay so I'm not the most well versed kn this topic, but you should be able to convert yout kombucha culture into a jun culture by slowly switching from black tea to green tea across batches, abd the same with white cane sugar to honey. You will notice the flavor change, the carbonation change, and also the speed at which it ferments. Jun generally ferments much faster than kombucha, and has a lighter flavor. It also seems to perform better at lower temperatures than kombucha.

Because I use some green tea, and I like my ferment after 4-5 days, it may be due to my kombucha resembling jun a little bit. I'm not super sure, but I find the fast fermentation a bit suspicious, and I do think the green tea has something to do with it.

### More info about your setup specifically?

I use 50% assam black tea and 50% longjing green tea. At first I was using english breakfast instead, and I was adding red rooibos too, but I prefer the cleaner flavor I have now. I use cane sugar right now, but am wondering about other sugar sources. I personally like flavoring with blueberry and ginger a lot, but I also drink unflavored kombucha, I just usually add 1 tsp of sugar instead of fruit for carbonation.

My F1 is usually done after 4-5 days, and I bottle for 2-3 days. I started my kombucha culture from GT's unflavored kombucha but I added a bit of unflavored Huney Jun at some point too.
